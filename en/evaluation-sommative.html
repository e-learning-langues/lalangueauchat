Summative assessment

Can we still assess a student’s work? The line between authentic and augmented production has become blurred. Yet by adapting our criteria, we have continued to assess students in asynchronous online courses: in writing, by valuing the process and critical thinking rather than the finished product alone; in speaking, by focusing on the personal, the creative and the spontaneous.

Written production, task type 1

Taking a critical look to do better: creating a flyer to promote a multicultural event

Dream Year Abroad · English B1+

Learning objective:

Assess students’ understanding of the resources and concepts covered in the course through a personal piece of writing that draws on GenAI to go beyond the initial output.

What the student does:

  • Assess the GenAI output: Analyse the flyer produced by ChatGPT and grade it using the rubric of criteria provided.

    Two ways to start

    Ask a chatbot to produce the flyer, or read the example of a GenAI-generated flyer provided in the course. This second option allows students who do not wish to use GenAI to do the activity.

  • Create their own flyer: Design their own flyer, incorporating the materials studied in the unit, to make it more creative, nuanced and engaging.
  • Self-assess: Grade their own work with the same rubric and explain how it is better than ChatGPT’s, justifying the grade given.

Written production, task type 2

Writing independently, comparing one’s work with GenAI’s and improving it: a guided self-correction process

Intercultural Communication · English B2+/C1

Learning objective:

Assess writing autonomy, critical thinking and command of the intercultural concepts covered in the course, through a multi-step process. In a first draft written without GenAI, the student makes a cognitive effort and leaves a record of their skills at a given moment. GenAI is not a starting point but a revealer that brings to light gaps the student might not have noticed on their own.

What the student does:

  • Write independently: Answer the question independently (≈ 350 words), without help from GenAI or machine translation.
  • Assess, then compare: Grade ChatGPT’s answer against the criteria, then compare it with their own work to identify differences and areas for improvement.

    Two ways to obtain ChatGPT’s answer

    The answer is generated by the student, or provided by the teacher if the student does not wish to use GenAI.

  • Revise: Produce an enriched final version that incorporates the missing elements, documenting any use of GenAI where relevant.

Spoken production, task type 1

Presenting the campus outdoors

Dream Year Abroad · English B1+

Learning objective:

Assess what has been learnt in the unit and the quality of speaking through videos in asynchronous online courses, without banning the use of GenAI for preparation but by requiring a personal production that the tool cannot do in the student’s place.

What the student does:

  • Reuse the course material on American campuses in a spoken production
  • Film themselves outdoors, walking around to present different places they care about on one of the USPN campuses
  • Use the language naturally
  • Use post-production tools (editing, music, effects, subtitles…) if they consider it necessary

Spoken production, task type 2

Sharing tips and tricks in a YouTube-style video

Life Hacks · English A2+

Learning objective:

Assess spoken production in the age of GenAI in asynchronous online courses, without banning its use and by requiring an original, playful and creative production. Students make short, dynamic and interactive videos, adopting the formats, hooks and aesthetics of social media. Facing the camera, they carry out concrete actions and share their immediate impressions, which encourages authenticity and spontaneity. Immersion in a real situation leads them to use targeted language structures fluently, far from rigid academic formats.

What the student does:

  • Present a tip related to the theme of the unit, taking on the role of a content creator.
  • Speak and film themselves while performing and commenting on practical actions (e.g. making a tutorial on how to chop an onion without crying)
  • Use the language naturally
  • Use post-production tools (editing, music, effects, subtitles…) if they consider it necessary

Assessment rubric (designed with GenAI)

The assessment rubric specifically rewards fluency, spontaneity and authenticity of live expression.

What we observe, limitations and avenues

What do we observe in writing?

  • Most use GenAI, but this is hard to measure: most students seem to use GenAI at some point in the improvement process, though this cannot be verified with certainty.
  • GenAI and layout: few students used the PowerPoint template provided, and many are comfortable enough with the tool to entrust part of the visual design to it.
  • Authenticity hard to verify: it is impossible to know for sure whether the first draft, the assessment of ChatGPT’s output and the self-assessment are original work or generated by GenAI. Many seem to hand the entire process over to GenAI, while others appear to do the work on their own.
  • Difficulties with the instructions: some students get lost in the sequence of steps and leave out parts of the task, such as documenting the process, because this kind of task is unfamiliar; others give a grade without writing an analysis. However, the ability to follow instructions has improved over the years, which suggests that students are getting used to these new forms of assessment.

What do we observe in speaking?

Students can still be assessed, even if the assessment criteria need to be adapted. We have seen strong personal involvement from students who, although they may use GenAI to help write their scripts, make a real effort to prepare.

  • A lever for creativity: Faced with an open task, students show imagination and humour to enrich their productions, readily using anecdotes, props and acting.
  • Command of contemporary content-creation codes: Students naturally adopt the codes of social media to present their experiments in an original and playful way.
  • Self-initiated post-production efforts: Without being asked, they make extensive use of tools to give their work a professional finish:
    • Automatic subtitling.
    • Editing and visual effects.
    • Sound design.

These efforts are all the more appreciated as the tasks are part of courses that are often optional and carry little weight in the overall grade. Watching these videos, each one unique, is a pleasure that lets the markers discover their students’ personalities. Motivation remains.

Limitations

Elaborate post-production can mask weak speaking. A visually polished, well-edited and subtitled video can give an impression of competence that does not reflect the actual quality of the spoken production.

The line between authentic and augmented production remains invisible. We cannot tell which parts of the final output are the student’s own work and which have been generated or heavily transformed by a tool.

GenAI can be used to describe the use of GenAI. Some students use these tools not only to produce the assessed content, but also to write the parts meant to document their own approach: description of the process, self-assessment, justification of choices. What was meant to be a space for personal reflection is delegated.

These findings call into question the format of asynchronous written assessment. If the authenticity of the work cannot be guaranteed in writing, the answer is to switch to oral assessment, where the student’s presence and voice are irreplaceable. This does, however, raise questions about feasibility on a large scale and about the e-learning model as it exists today.

The value of effort is also being questioned. If GenAI can complete an entire task in a few seconds, what motivates students to put in the work? These shifts invite us to rethink not only assessment methods but also the very foundations of learning: what we ask students to do, why we ask them to do it, and what skills the effort actually builds.

Avenues we are exploring

As soon as the level allows, have students critique GenAI feedback. Ask students to submit their own work to get feedback, then to analyse and critique that feedback. Spotting what the tool over-corrects, overlooks or prioritises poorly becomes an exercise in critical thinking.

Make the use of GenAI visible. Ask students to document the tools they used and explain their choices in a video. Showing how GenAI is used then becomes a learning objective in itself, rather than a grey area to be policed.

Favour speaking, synchronous or asynchronous.
Move part of the assessment to formats that involve the student’s voice, face and spontaneity (face-to-camera videos, presentations by videoconference or in person).

Include an oral defence or discussion.
Follow a written production with an oral exchange in which the student explains their choices and answers questions. If the work is really their own, they will be able to talk about it; if not, the gap shows.

Demo and challenge

Demo

At the conference “L’intelligence artificielle générative dans les pratiques de formation et dans la recherche : enjeux et défis” (Generative artificial intelligence in training practices and research: issues and challenges; MEDIALECT, Université Sorbonne Paris Nord, 1st, 2nd and 3rd October 2026), this demo presented three student videos. The students had agreed to them being shown on that occasion only, so they are no longer online. Here is what they showed.

  • Presenting your universityIn the Dream Year Abroad course, a student had to present USPN, in two to three minutes, to international students about to arrive. Camera in hand, he walks around the campus: buildings, places to eat and meet up, administration, cultural services, sports facilities, the Espace Langues.
  • Chopping onions without cryingIn the Life Hacks course, after watching a series of videos to learn the useful words and structures, a student tests three methods of chopping onions without crying in front of the camera. She demonstrates and explains each one, then says which worked best and what gave her trouble. The instructions made it clear: crying was allowed.
  • Viral hacks put to the testAlso in Life Hacks, the same student picks several hacks spotted online, such as the toilet-roll smartphone speaker or the crispy microwave pizza crust. She tests them, noting what does not work, then presents it all in a two-minute video, full of humour, in which she explains why each hack failed to live up to its promise.

Case study

Example from the “Career” module

Life Hacks · English A2+

Scenario

Learners are asked to create a fictional CV for one of the weirdest jobs in the world (see the Written production station) and post it on a forum. We used GenAI to give them detailed feedback on this ungraded activity, with the tutor’s role being to check its reliability.

“Career” example, continued (1/2)

Example of feedback written by GenAI

  • Job Choice: You chose a very creative and "weird" job. It fits the instructions perfectly.
  • Structure: The CV is well-organized with clear sections for Profile, Work Experience, Education, and Skills.
  • Humor: The content is funny but stays professional in its tone, which is a great balance for this exercise.
  • Skills Section: You followed the instructions by dividing your skills into three specific parts.
  • Vocabulary: You used specific verbs related to the job like "sewing", "stuffing", and "dyeing". This is very good for your level.
  • Grammar: Your use of dates and time periods is correct.
  • Small Mistakes : Watch out for some spelling errors like "opperate" (should be operate) or "surgon" (should be surgeon). Also, "an another" should just be another.

“Career” example, continued (2/2)

Conclusion

Our sample is very small, as few students hand in the optional written productions. However, the main pitfall is GenAI’s tendency to dwell on purely cosmetic points (suggesting one word rather than another without any real justification) and, conversely, to overlook more serious errors: GenAI does not distinguish between what matters and what does not.

It remains essential for the teacher to assess the production as well. GenAI feedback does not replace the human connection, nor the teacher’s ability to encourage, to put errors in context, and to tailor their feedback to each student’s personal path and progress.
