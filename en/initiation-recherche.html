First steps in research… in the original language

Academic research in English is just a few prompts away. The point is not to produce quickly but to learn to question, check and critique what the machine suggests.

Defining a research question and exploring the literature with GenAI

Intercultural Communication · English · week 3 “What is culture?”
  • Formulate a question: Starting from their field of interest, ask a chatbot to suggest research questions, then choose one and adapt it.
  • Explore the literature: Ask it for a synthesis of existing research on the topic (articles, books, sources) with an APA-style bibliography, then check that each reference exists (university library catalogue, Google Scholar).
  • Refine with the FINER criteria: Test the question against the FINER criteria (feasible, interesting, novel, ethical, relevant), ask for suggestions for improvement, and try other prompts to strengthen it.

Summarising a research article before reading it

Intercultural Communication · English · week 6 “The Dimensions of Culture”
  • Find an article: Identify an article that may be useful for their research, using the university library’s online catalogue if needed.
  • Ask for a summary: Submit the article to a chatbot and ask it to produce a summary of a set length.
  • Decide: Based on the summary, judge whether the article deserves a close reading, bearing in mind that the GenAI summary may be incomplete or inaccurate.

Comparing registers

Intercultural Communication · English · week 8 “Melting Pots vs Mosaics”
  • Choose an article: Select an article and submit it to a chatbot.
  • Generate 3 versions: Ask the chatbot for a 300-word summary of the same article in three successive registers: informal (to a friend, conversational tone), neutral (factual, for a general audience) and scientific (technical, formal, aimed at researchers).
  • Compare and analyse: Set the 3 summaries side by side to spot what changes from one register to another (vocabulary, tone, structure) and draw reusable pointers for their own writing.

Assessing an article in a peer reviewer’s shoes

Intercultural Communication · English · week 9 “Cultural Appropriation versus Cultural Appreciation”
  • Choose the right rubric: Identify the type of article to be assessed and select the appropriate set of criteria from the four provided.
  • Run the review: Ask the chatbot to take on the role of a specialist researcher and to review the article as a peer reviewer would, strictly applying the chosen criteria.
  • Analyse the recommendation: Examine the strengths, weaknesses and decision (accept or reject the article) put forward by GenAI, keeping a critical eye on the relevance and rigour of its assessment.

Translating an abstract and its keywords

Intercultural Communication · English · week 10 “Healthcare and Culture”
  • Submit the abstract: Copy the article’s English abstract and ask a chatbot to translate it into French.
  • Get the keywords: Ask the chatbot to select ten keywords in French from the translated abstract.
  • Check and correct: Reread the translation and keywords with a critical eye, check that the specialist terminology of the field is accurate and idiomatic in French, then correct whatever needs correcting.

Finding sources: Boolean search vs GenAI

Expansion (M2 LEPS, SMBH) · English · module “Research with AI”
  • Write a Boolean query: Choose a topic and build a search (AND/OR) in PubMed or Google Scholar (standard search, without Scholar Labs mode).
  • Query a GenAI assistant: Run the same search again with a tool such as Elicit, Consensus or SciSpace.
  • Compare the results: Assess the number, relevance and quality of the sources found by each method.

Paraphrasing a scientific text without plagiarising

Expansion (M2 LEPS, SMBH) · English · module “Research with AI”
  • Choose a source text: Select a text to paraphrase.
  • Test several tools: Have different GenAI tools paraphrase the same passage (QuillBot, Mizou, ChatGPT, Claude…).
  • Assess the quality: Compare the resulting versions for clarity, faithfulness to the meaning, originality and risk of plagiarism.

Getting a graph analysed

Expansion (M2 LEPS, SMBH) · English · module “Research with AI”
  • Learn to describe graphs in English: Identify the vocabulary and structures used to describe a trend in English.
  • Submit the infographic to a chatbot: Upload the graph and ask for an analysis of the trends and an interpretation.
  • Check the analysis: Compare the response with the original infographic to spot omissions, errors or hallucinations.

Generating an abstract that follows a standard structure

Expansion (M2 LEPS, SMBH) · English · module “Research with AI”
  • Identify the target type of abstract: Following the module on abstract structure, use examples to identify the type of abstract expected (structured, semi-structured…) by a publication.
  • Generate an abstract: Write a precise prompt based on an article without an abstract, refining it until satisfied.
  • Compare with the original: Set the generated version against the actual abstract (missing information, style, hallucinations).

Creating slides for a conference presentation

Expansion (M2 LEPS, SMBH) · English · module “Research with AI”
  • Learn about good practice: Identify, in the examples given, the principles of an effective slide for a scientific presentation.
  • Generate the slides: Import the content of their presentation into a GenAI tool (Gamma or similar) and adjust the layout and images.
  • Assess the tool’s added value: Judge whether the tool highlighted the right information and saved time.

Demo and challenge

Demo

The liAIsons project

Rethinking our teaching practices is no longer optional: it is essential to keep them relevant and to sustain students’ motivation. This is why we launched the liAIsons project.

We identified priority research areas and drew on GenAI’s processing capabilities to carry out a comprehensive review of several teaching-related topics, making particular use of its ability to analyse large volumes of scientific data. This work is intended to feed our collective reflection and inform our decision-making.

Keen to make this research accessible to as many people as possible, we also used GenAI to share the findings as popular-science podcasts, available on a dedicated YouTube channel and Spotify show.

However, the data generated by GenAI carry a margin of error, as the technology is prone to hallucinations that are hard to spot without specialist expertise. Although we put a rigorous protocol in place (systematic cross-checking and spot checks), some inaccuracies may have escaped our notice. Despite these limitations, we remain convinced that this approach is worthwhile and could serve as a model in other settings, particularly with students (learning to fact-check, writing dissertations, etc.).

What about translation?

Very good quality for some language pairs and domains. For “high-resource” languages such as the French–English pair and standardised content (abstracts, technical instructions), quality sometimes comes close to human translation.

Overall meaning and basic vocabulary are preserved. LLMs handle general vocabulary and some scientific terminology well, which makes them excellent tools for extracting the main ideas and for pre-drafting.

Hallucinations and fabrications. LLMs can invent elements that are not in the source text (references, figures, wording), sometimes plausible but factually wrong.

Bias and discursive framing. These systems reflect the biases in their training data (gender, culture, scientific paradigms), which can subtly alter the way social or ethical issues are framed.

Failure on stylistically distinctive texts. With a long, original or literary text, LLMs miss nuances, implicit meanings and stylistic effects.

As soon as writing departs from standardised forms, translation collapses: the surface meaning comes through, but the author’s voice is lost.

Avenues we are exploring

Comparing several tools on the same task.
Submit the same prompt to different chatbots (Le Chat, ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude…) and compare the results (differences in translation, summary, assessment…). The discrepancies make GenAI’s instability visible and force users to exercise their own judgement rather than trust a single output.

GenAI–human collaboration and literacy in GenAI-assisted translation.

  • Approach translation as a three-stage process:
    1. generation;
    2. expert post-editing (teacher, translator, researcher);
    3. collective validation, with roles and responsibilities made explicit.
  • Build into language courses activities comparing GenAI and human translations, analysing translation errors (terminology, nuance, bias) and guided rewriting.

But if GenAI can do everything, what becomes of research?

For a start, GenAI cannot do everything! This means that, if we want to assess dissertations, we need to check that students have a real command of the content they explored, with or without GenAI. We can assess the research process, the accuracy of the sources and, orally, the students’ in-depth command of what they have written…
