Training students in GenAI and leading by example

Students who object are never required to use GenAI.

Providing user guides

Since January 2023, we have provided students with various teaching materials, in text or video form, to support them in learning about and getting to grips with generative AI tools.

  • The first guide focused on the fundamentals of large language models (LLMs) and on concrete examples of prompt engineering applied to language learning (such as simulating interviews, creating correction grids or simplifying texts for a general audience).
  • In the following ones, we raised the level of complexity of the uses and offered more variety in the language practice activities, but we also presented the risks: hallucinations, bias, environmental impact, data protection, cognitive atrophy.

Telling students what was produced with GenAI

In our courses, after an initial period during which we did not think to flag that some content had been generated with the help of GenAI, we decided to make this use visible to students.

Showing the prompts

It is possible to:

  • reveal the prompts,
  • hide them in a clipboard (students will then see the prompt when they paste it),
  • not reveal them, by using a Gem or a GPT.

In our courses and on the Espace Langues self-directed learning site, we chose to reveal the prompts. Indeed, not all students know how to “prompt”, and some say they appreciate seeing how the requests used to interact with GenAI in a foreign language are written, so that they can write their own in other contexts.

Encouraging reflection within courses

Integrating information on the issues raised by GenAI into courses, while working on the language.

Advantages and disadvantages

During the sessions in which students in exile on the DU Passerelle (French as a foreign language, FLE) get to grips with the tools, including chatbots, a reading comprehension activity is offered, in which students have to work in teams to reconstruct a text on the advantages and disadvantages of GenAI.

Environmental impact

To prepare for the CLES B2 (French university language certification), students simulate an application to be a youth representative at the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP). They write a report and then debate the question: “Do the benefits of generative AI in tackling climate change justify its environmental cost?”, an approach that makes them concretely aware of the carbon footprint of digital technology.

For or against GenAI for learning

In TrèFLE, the online FLE course of the DU Passerelle, B1-level students reflect on the most effective teaching methods, from the flipped classroom to project-based learning, by way of e-learning and the use of GenAI. In the DELF-style speaking task, students argue against or in favour of GenAI for learning, having tried it out for themselves.

Critical reflection in English

Evaluating tools for research

Every three weeks, M2 LEPS students are invited to use a chatbot or another GenAI tool to carry out a research-related activity.

In the questionnaire for that period, they are asked to evaluate the tools they used, which allows them to compare the advantages of GenAI with human added value while practising written production in English in this ungraded activity.

Creativity and critical thinking

To develop prompt engineering skills and stimulate critical thinking, we asked each student to take on a double challenge of writing and evaluation.

  • STEP 1: Having studied and analysed the different types of humour, the student chooses a specific type of humour and has ChatGPT generate a humorous text in the first person. The script must be exactly 150 words long and must include 5 words chosen from a list provided. Particular attention is paid to the precision of the instructions given to GenAI in order to obtain a convincing result.
  • STEP 2: After posting the generated script in the shared space, the student must write a 150-word critical analysis, in English, giving their opinion on ChatGPT’s output (compliance with the constraints, effectiveness of the comic tone, limitations of the machine). Students are then invited to read and comment on their peers’ scripts.

The result makes it possible to test straight away the effectiveness of precise instructions (prompts) applied to a complex and nuanced register. Students notice that, without extremely precise and iterative instructions, ChatGPT tends to produce standardised, literal or “clichéd” humour.

What students mainly take away is that humour remains a human affair: when well steered and reviewed, GenAI can imitate a comic register, but on this ground it does not replace us.

Asking students for their views

Student feedback on GenAI in online language courses (Espace Langues, SEG, LEPS: 2024-2026)

In terms of self-reported mastery of the tools, technical vocabulary remains unstable: the categories “agent” and “chatbot”, although distinct, are for example used interchangeably, which reflects a familiarity of use that is still rarely backed by conceptual reference points.

In terms of the outputs generated by GenAI, students identify concrete limitations: errors, mistranslations, overly literal translations, variability between tools… especially when the task asks them to compare the GenAI output with a reference.

These forms of critical thinking coexist. No course contains them all to the same degree:

  • The agent/chatbot confusion is visible in the answers of the SEG bachelor’s students. Doubts about reliability emerge mainly among the M2 public health students, who were comparing translations. Ecological concern is expressed mainly by the M2 public health students, and occasionally in SEG L2 and in Life Hacks. Principled refusal and attachment to human authenticity are expressed mainly in Intercultural Communication and Life Hacks.
  • Critical thinking about GenAI is not a stable, acquired skill but a disposition that the very design of the task can either call upon or leave dormant.
    • When a task makes the limitations of GenAI visible, for example by asking students to compare several machine translations of a scientific text with a reference translation, critical reflection is awakened.
    • When the task does not create this opportunity, the same student may well say nothing critical, not because they are unable to, but because nothing prompted them to.

Some students articulate a principled stance: ecological concern, rejection of what they see as excessive delegation, attachment to authenticity and personal creativity.

What is at stake is the capacity of instructional design to create the conditions for critical distance.

Training teachers

The Espace Langues is also active in training teachers of LANSAD (languages for students of other disciplines), languages and LLSHS (humanities and social sciences).

Several introductory sessions and workshops have been organised since 2023.

Teachers are not only getting to know the tools: they are seeking to rethink teaching in the age of GenAI, drawing on student feedback (see in particular the ongoing LLSHS seminar).
